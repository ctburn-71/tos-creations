The Vision — Before Development Began

Breakpoint Run began with a question: What if the entire city became a racecourse?

Not a traditional racing game with vehicles, and not a runner locked to one predetermined path.

Players would race from one side of a sprawling city to the other, choosing how they got there.

One player might sprint down streets, weaving past construction zones and roadblocks. Another might climb to a rooftop, cross between buildings, then drop into an alley to find a faster shortcut.

Skill, exploration, timing, and creativity would all matter.

Sprinting, jumping, sliding, vaulting, climbing, and eventually wall-running would open up different routes.

From the beginning, the primary focus was iPhone and Android. The ambition is realistic urban action with responsive, approachable touchscreen controls—not a separate button for every possible movement.

Detailed architecture will come later. First, the city must be exciting to move through.

The city is the obstacle. The route is your choice. The clock is your competition.

The Weekly Development Journey

Explore the early experiments, challenges, and milestones as Breakpoint Run grows from a concept into a playable escape experience.

Week 1 — November 15–21, 2026
Building the City and Bringing Movement to Life

November 15 — From the First City Block to the First Escape

Development began in Unreal Engine 5.8 with the creation of the BreakPointRun project.

The first task was preparing Unreal on Mac and connecting OpenAI Codex to the editor through Unreal's Model Context Protocol integration.

This established the working approach: plan the gameplay, implement it in the engine, test it directly, and refine what does not yet feel right.

Building the First City

A new level, Lvl_CityTraversal_Blockout, was created while preserving Unreal's original First Person starter environment.

The first playable city blockout included:

  • Four connected city blocks
  • Eight usable buildings with rooftop access
  • Streets, sidewalks, and alleys
  • Rooftop bridges, scaffolding, stairs, and terraces
  • Roadblocks, barriers, and vaulting obstacles
  • Alternative ground-level and rooftop routes
  • A designated starting area and finish zone

The structures intentionally used simple development geometry.

The goal was not yet a beautiful city; it was a city worth running through.

Introducing Speed and Race Timing

A dedicated Breakpoint Run character and game mode were created.

Sprinting, momentum settings, and camera adjustments began establishing a sense of speed.

The race timer connected the starting point to the escape zone, making it possible to complete a course and compete against previous times.

The first successful manually tested run finished in 33.15 seconds.

An early subsequent run improved to 16.19 seconds.

The first gameplay loop was working: escape, learn the route, and try again faster.

Developing the First Parkour Mechanics

The first movement improvements included:

  • Adjustable camera sensitivity
  • Improved jumping controls
  • Crouching beneath low obstacles
  • Momentum-based sliding
  • Context-sensitive obstacle vaulting
  • Ledge catching and short mantling
  • Collision and landing-safety checks
  • Preservation of movement speed and race timing

Early tests showed that the controls needed to remain intuitive.

The Spacebar was adapted to respond to the player's surroundings: jumping in open space, vaulting suitable low obstacles, or mantling a reachable ledge.

This is an early foundation for future context-sensitive touchscreen controls, not a finished mobile control scheme.

Testing and Refining the Experience

Repeated manual testing identified overly sensitive mouse movement, jumping issues, and opportunities to improve crouching, sliding, vaulting, and mantling.

Adjustments were made while keeping the race timer operational.

Unreal's memory demands also highlighted the need to monitor editor resource usage and prioritize mobile performance as the environment grows.

The First Day's Milestone

By the end of November 15, Breakpoint Run had become an early playable parkour racing prototype.

The city could be traversed, the character could run, jump, crouch, slide, vault, and mantle, and a start-to-finish escape could be timed.

Wall-running, richer rooftop traversal, realistic buildings, mobile controls, and testing on physical phones remain future work.

One day. One city. The first successful escape. And a much bigger journey ahead.

November 17–18 — Transforming the City

With the first playable city established, development turned toward creating a believable modern American downtown rather than a collection of gray blockout structures.

Architectural improvements included:

  • Brick apartment buildings with detailed windows and entrances
  • Commercial storefronts, awnings, and signage
  • Modern offices and industrial architecture
  • Rooftop equipment, railings, and architectural details
  • Sidewalks, curbs, crosswalks, and road markings
  • Streetlights, traffic signals, and urban fixtures
  • Improved building materials, textures, and weathering

The first district received its architectural update before the approved visual treatment expanded across the remaining three playable blocks.

Testing revealed that a city could look detailed from above but still feel empty from street level.

Surrounding buildings, additional streets, varied rooflines, parked vehicles, and distant skyline structures were introduced to make the playable course feel part of a larger city.

The first eastern city sightline was completed and approved as a foundation for expansion.

The goal is a believable city that preserves the freedom to choose streets, rooftops, walls, and alternate routes.

November 18 — A New Development Home

As the city became more detailed, Unreal Engine's resource demands grew.

Development had begun on a Mac with 16 GB of memory. Larger scenes, shader processing, and editor operations occasionally created memory pressure and instability.

The project was transferred between two Macs using local network file sharing.

The new primary development machine became a 14-inch MacBook Pro with an Apple M6 processor and 32 GB of unified memory.

Unreal Engine 5.8.4 successfully opened the transferred project. The city, traversal routes, character movement, and race timer were verified.

OpenAI Codex was reconnected through Unreal's Model Context Protocol integration.

The move was completed without rebuilding the project or losing the existing city.

November 18 — Bringing the Runner to Life

Breakpoint Run had initially been experienced primarily from a first-person perspective.

That changed with the introduction of Manny, a visible animated character using Unreal Engine's existing character assets.

The third-person foundation introduced:

  • A visible full-body player character
  • A camera following behind the runner
  • Animated walking and running
  • Jumping, falling, and landing animations
  • Integration with existing parkour movement
  • Camera refinements for comfortable control
  • Preserved race timing and finish detection

For the first time, players could watch Manny navigate the city rather than simply experience movement through the camera.

Making the City Safe to Explore

Early third-person testing revealed several important environmental problems.

Manny could move beyond an unprotected city boundary and fall into empty space.

Invisible boundaries, a safety surface, and automatic recovery were introduced. Falling outside the playable area now returns the character to the starting location without stopping the race timer.

Additional testing found decorative buildings with missing collision, allowing Manny to pass through solid-looking walls.

Targeted collision repairs corrected the affected structures while preserving the established parkour routes.

A believable environment must not only look realistic. It must behave correctly when players interact with it.

Making Movement Feel Natural

With Manny visible, development focused on making the character's actions look as natural as they felt during gameplay.

Animation improvements included:

  • Speed-responsive sprinting
  • Stationary crouching
  • Crouched walking
  • Momentum-based sliding
  • Smoother transitions between movement states
  • Preserved jumping and landing animations

Manual review revealed that the first crouch-walking animation had unnatural leg movement and noticeable foot drifting.

The animation was rebuilt with an improved stride, steadier positioning, and better foot movement.

The revised crouch-walk passed personal review.

Visible Vaulting, Ledge Catching, and Mantling

The next animation stage focused on moving across obstacles rather than simply running around them.

Visible vaulting, ledge-catching, and mantling animations were introduced.

One early problem prevented Manny from visibly reaching for a ledge while airborne.

Investigation showed that ledge detection was occurring only when the jump button was first pressed.

Detection was refined so that holding the jump button allowed Manny to recognize and catch a reachable ledge during the jump.

A dedicated test ledge was added to verify the interaction.

Manny could now jump toward a ledge, reach out, catch it, pull himself onto the surface, and return to running.

Learning to Run on Walls

Development then moved to one of the game's more ambitious movement features: wall-running.

Dedicated left-side and right-side testing lanes were created with approach ramps, gaps, walls, and landing areas.

The first implementation required the character to build speed, jump alongside a suitable wall, and maintain forward movement.

Although initial automated checks passed, manual testing found activation too difficult.

Approach distances, angles, and speed requirements were adjusted to make wall-running easier to trigger without introducing additional controls.

The next challenge was making the movement visually believable.

Early versions caused Manny to glide along the wall or intersect its surface.

Improvements introduced:

  • Better body orientation and wall alignment
  • More natural leaning
  • Alternating leg movement
  • Improved foot placement
  • Lightweight foot-contact adjustments
  • Smoother animation transitions
  • More realistic running cadence
  • Corrections for arm clipping during tested exits

Several manual reviews were needed. At one point, Manny's legs were moving much too quickly to look natural.

Animation timing was gradually adjusted until the movement became more believable.

By the end of November 18, the provisional left-side and right-side wall-running foundation had been approved for continued development.

November 18 — A Playable Foundation Takes Shape

By November 18, Breakpoint Run had progressed well beyond its original gray city blockout.

Its playable foundation included a more detailed four-block urban environment, surrounding skyline structures, a visible third-person runner, timed escape gameplay, and multiple forms of parkour traversal.

Players could experience running, jumping, crouching, sliding, vaulting, ledge catching, mantling, and provisional wall-running.

Personal gameplay testing continued to reveal problems that automated checks alone could not fully capture.

Animation quality, collision behavior, street-level immersion, and the feel of each movement remained essential parts of the development process.

Mobile controls, physical-device performance testing, further city expansion, and additional animation refinement remain ahead.

By November 18, Breakpoint Run was no longer simply a city being constructed. It was becoming a city worth running through.

Future Plans

Breakpoint Run's next stages will continue refining the experience of racing through a believable modern city.

Development will focus on improving the realism of character animations, expanding wall-running opportunities, and making movement between streets, rooftops, and vertical routes increasingly fluid.

Architectural improvements will continue throughout the city while preserving the alternate routes and obstacles that make each race interesting.

Mobile remains the primary platform focus. Touchscreen controls, graphics optimization, and physical-device testing are important upcoming milestones.

Each improvement will be implemented, tested, and reviewed before becoming part of the larger gameplay experience.

The challenge is not simply to finish. It is to learn the city, discover faster routes, and improve with every attempt.
